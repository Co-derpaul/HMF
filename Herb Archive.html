01 Bitter Leaf Onugbu (Igbo), Ewuro (Yoruba), Shakikai (Hausa) Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

A staple of West African compound gardens for generations, bitter leaf earned its place in the kitchen long before it earned one in the pharmacy, prized for turning the famous bitterleaf soup into a household remedy.

Where to Find It

Grows readily in home gardens and farmland across southern and central Nigeria; sold fresh in bundles or pre-washed and shredded at almost every local market.

Preparation

Fresh leaves are washed repeatedly (traditionally squeezed in water) to reduce bitterness, or dried and ground into powder; a cooled leaf-water infusion is also common.

How to Use

Drink the washed-leaf infusion on an empty stomach, or cook the leaves into soup; the powder can be stirred into warm water or capsulated.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Supports healthy blood sugar levels
  • Traditionally used for stomach upsets and worms
  • Rich in antioxidants and vitamins A & C
  • Used to support liver and digestive health

Side Effects & Cautions

  • May lower blood sugar too far if combined with diabetes medication
  • Excessive intake can cause stomach discomfort or loose stools

Good to Know

Diabetics on medication should monitor blood sugar closely when using bitter leaf, as the combined effect can be stronger than expected.

02 Moringa Ewe Igbale (Yoruba), Zogale (Hausa), Okwe-oyibo (Igbo) Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Known globally as the 'miracle tree', moringa was adopted into Nigerian herbal practice as its nutrient density became widely recognised, and it now grows in nearly every region.

Where to Find It

A fast-growing, drought-tolerant tree found in backyards, roadside plantings, and increasingly on dedicated moringa farms in the north and southwest.

Preparation

Leaves are sun-dried in shade and ground into a fine green powder, or used fresh in soups; seeds are pressed for oil.

How to Use

Stir a teaspoon of the powder into water, tea, pap, or smoothies daily; fresh leaves can be cooked like spinach.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Very high in vitamins, iron and protein
  • Traditionally used to support breast-milk production
  • Used for general fatigue and low energy
  • Believed to support healthy blood pressure

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Root and bark extracts (not leaves) can be harmful and are avoided
  • Very large doses may act as a mild laxative

Good to Know

Pregnant women are traditionally advised to use only the leaf, not the root or bark, which are treated very differently in herbal practice.

07 Scent Leaf Nchanwu (Igbo), Efirin (Yoruba), Daidoya (Hausa) Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

A fragrant garden herb woven into everyday Igbo and Yoruba cooking, scent leaf's aroma made it as much a kitchen seasoning as a home remedy for stomach and cold complaints.

Where to Find It

Grows easily in home gardens across the south; sold fresh in bunches at virtually every local food market.

Preparation

Fresh leaves are used whole in cooking, or crushed and steeped in hot water for tea; the juice can be extracted by pounding.

How to Use

Add torn leaves to soups near the end of cooking, chew a few fresh leaves, or drink as a warm infusion for stomach upset.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally eases stomach upset and diarrhoea
  • Used for coughs and catarrh
  • Believed to support blood sugar balance
  • Freshens breath and is used for mouth odour

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Very large amounts may cause mild stomach irritation
  • Limited safety data for long-term heavy use in pregnancy

Good to Know

Scent leaf is often the first home remedy reached for when a child has an upset stomach, cooked directly into pepper soup.

10 Utazi Utazi (Igbo), Arokeke (Yoruba), Gongronema latifolium Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

A intensely bitter climbing vine leaf central to Igbo cuisine, utazi has long doubled as a tonic for new mothers and anyone needing a digestive 'reset'.

Where to Find It

Cultivated as a garden climber across the south-east and south-south; sold fresh, finely shredded, in local markets.

Preparation

Fresh leaves are shredded very finely (its bitterness is potent) and used sparingly, or the juice is squeezed out for direct use.

How to Use

Sprinkle finely shredded leaf into soups like ofe nsala, or take a small amount of the juice diluted in water.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally supports blood sugar regulation
  • Used to ease stomach upset and improve appetite
  • Believed to support liver health
  • Used postpartum to help the womb contract

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Its intense bitterness can cause nausea if used in excess
  • May lower blood sugar significantly in diabetics on medication

Good to Know

Because it's so bitter, utazi is traditionally used in small, carefully measured amounts rather than as a bulk vegetable.

14 Neem Dogoyaro / Dogonyaro (Hausa-derived, widely used nationally) Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Introduced from Asia generations ago, neem naturalised so thoroughly across Nigeria that 'dogoyaro' became a household name for its intensely bitter, fever-fighting leaves.

Where to Find It

A common shade tree found along roadsides and compound fences nationwide, especially in the north and Middle Belt; leaves are free for the picking.

Preparation

Fresh or dried leaves are boiled to make a strongly bitter tea; leaves can also be pounded for topical paste.

How to Use

Drink the bitter leaf-tea for fever or malaria symptoms, or apply pounded leaf paste to skin rashes and itches.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Widely used as a traditional remedy for malaria and fever
  • Applied topically for skin conditions and itching
  • Believed to have antimicrobial and antiparasitic action
  • Used in some traditional detox and worming regimens

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Very bitter and can cause nausea or stomach upset
  • Not recommended in pregnancy or for young infants
  • Should not replace prescribed antimalarial treatment for confirmed malaria

Good to Know

Neem tea is a very common first home response to fever, but it is not a substitute for a confirmed malaria diagnosis and proper antimalarial treatment.

19 Cashew Leaf Ewe Kaju (Yoruba), Anacardium occidentale Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Introduced by Portuguese traders centuries ago, the cashew tree naturalised across Nigeria, and its bitter young leaves became a folk remedy long before the nut became a snack export.

Where to Find It

Common in orchards and compound gardens especially in the Middle Belt and south-west; young leaves are picked directly from the tree.

Preparation

Young, tender leaves are boiled to make a bitter tea; the bark is sometimes boiled similarly.

How to Use

Drink the cooled leaf tea, traditionally for stomach upset, fever, or as a mouth rinse for oral discomfort.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally used for diarrhoea and stomach upset
  • Used as a mouth rinse for gum and tooth discomfort
  • Believed to help reduce fever
  • Some traditional use for blood sugar support

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Very bitter and may cause mild nausea if drunk in excess
  • Limited formal safety data for long-term use

Good to Know

Cashew leaf tea is a lesser-known but long-standing rural remedy, distinct from the popular cashew nut and apple.

20 Guava Leaf Ewe Gova (Yoruba), Guava Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Guava trees, introduced generations ago, spread into nearly every Nigerian backyard, and their leaves quickly became a trusted household remedy for upset stomachs.

Where to Find It

A common fruit tree in home compounds nationwide; fresh leaves are simply picked from the tree as needed.

Preparation

Fresh or slightly wilted leaves are boiled in water to make a firm, slightly astringent tea.

How to Use

Drink the warm tea for diarrhoea or stomach upset; the cooled tea can also be used as a mouth rinse.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Widely used as a home remedy for diarrhoea
  • Believed to support oral health when used as a rinse
  • Traditionally used to ease minor stomach cramps
  • Contains antioxidants that support general wellness

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Excessive use may cause constipation due to its astringency
  • Not a substitute for medical care in severe or prolonged diarrhoea

Good to Know

Guava leaf tea is one of the most reached-for remedies in Nigerian homes the moment a stomach bug strikes.

25 African Basil Efirin Nla / Efirin Aja (Yoruba), Nchanwu Ukwu (Igbo) Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

A larger, more pungent cousin of scent leaf, African basil has long been grown around Yoruba compounds both for cooking and for its reputation as a protective, cleansing herb.

Where to Find It

Grown in home gardens across the south-west; sold fresh in bundles at local markets.

Preparation

Fresh leaves are crushed for juice, steeped as tea, or added directly to food.

How to Use

Chew fresh leaves for stomach upset, drink the leaf tea for colds, or cook into soups and stews.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally used for stomach upset and bloating
  • Used for cough, cold and catarrh
  • Believed to have calming, stress-easing effects
  • Traditionally used in ritual and cleansing baths

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Very large amounts may cause mild stomach irritation
  • Essential-oil-strength extracts should be used cautiously in pregnancy

Good to Know

Beyond medicine, African basil holds cultural significance in Yoruba spiritual and cleansing practices, often used in ritual baths.

31 Waterleaf Gbure (Yoruba), Mgbolodi (Igbo), Talinum triangulare Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

A soft, succulent leafy vegetable grown across southern Nigeria, waterleaf became known both as an everyday soup vegetable and a gentle traditional cooling remedy.

Where to Find It

Easily grown in home gardens and widely cultivated as a vegetable crop in the south; sold fresh in every local market.

Preparation

Fresh leaves are washed and cooked lightly, or the juice extracted from crushed fresh leaves.

How to Use

Cook into soups such as edikang ikong or afang, or take the fresh juice in small amounts for a cooling effect.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Rich in iron and traditionally used to help with mild anaemia
  • Believed to have a cooling, soothing effect on the body
  • Supports healthy digestion
  • A good source of vitamins A and C

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Contains oxalates, so very large regular intake is best balanced with a varied diet
  • Should be cooked lightly rather than eaten raw in bulk

Good to Know

Waterleaf's mild, juicy texture makes it one of the gentlest and most beginner-friendly leafy vegetables in Nigerian cooking.

36 Cocoyam Leaf Ewe Koko (Yoruba), Ede Leaf (Igbo) Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Cocoyam has fed Nigerian families for generations, and its broad leaves — used in dishes like ekpang nkukwo — carry a smaller but real tradition as a wellness food.

Where to Find It

Grown widely as a root and leaf crop across the south; fresh leaves are sold in local vegetable markets.

Preparation

Leaves must be cooked thoroughly (never eaten raw) to neutralise natural irritants, typically simmered for an extended time.

How to Use

Cook thoroughly into dishes such as ekpang nkukwo or leaf soups, always with adequate cooking time.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • A good source of dietary fibre and vitamins
  • Traditionally considered a nourishing, energy-supporting food
  • Used as a wholesome addition to postpartum diets
  • Contributes iron and minerals to the diet

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Must be cooked thoroughly — raw or undercooked leaves contain irritant crystals that can cause itching and throat irritation
  • Not suitable for those with kidney stone concerns without medical guidance

Good to Know

Thorough, prolonged cooking is non-negotiable with cocoyam leaves; undercooked leaves are a common cause of throat and mouth irritation.

37 Fluted Pumpkin Leaf (Ugu) Ugu (Igbo/national), Telfairia occidentalis Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

One of Nigeria's most beloved leafy vegetables, ugu is grown almost everywhere in the south and has long been recommended to new mothers and the recovering as a blood-building food.

Where to Find It

Widely cultivated as a climbing vegetable across the south; sold fresh, chopped or whole, in every local market.

Preparation

Fresh leaves are washed and lightly cooked, or blended fresh for juice.

How to Use

Cook into soups such as ugu soup or egusi, or take the fresh juice in small amounts, especially postpartum.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally believed to support healthy blood levels and recovery from anaemia
  • Rich in iron, vitamins A and C
  • Used to support recovery after illness, surgery or childbirth
  • Believed to support healthy blood pressure

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Very high in vitamin K, which can interact with blood-thinning medication
  • Best cooked lightly rather than eaten raw in large amounts

Good to Know

Ugu is one of the most frequently recommended vegetables for new mothers and anyone recovering from blood loss or illness.

42 Cassava Leaf Ewe Gbaguda (Yoruba), Cassava/Manioc Leaf Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

While cassava root feeds much of Nigeria as garri and fufu, its leaves have a smaller but real tradition as a cooked vegetable and folk remedy in some regions.

Where to Find It

Grown wherever cassava is cultivated, which is nearly everywhere in the south and Middle Belt; leaves are gathered fresh from the plant.

Preparation

Leaves must be pounded and cooked thoroughly for an extended time to remove naturally occurring cyanogenic compounds.

How to Use

Cook thoroughly into a leaf soup or stew, ensuring long cooking time before eating.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • A source of dietary fibre and plant protein when properly prepared
  • Traditionally eaten as a nourishing vegetable in some regions
  • Contains vitamins A and C after proper cooking
  • Used in some folk traditions to support recovery from illness

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Must be thoroughly cooked — improperly prepared leaves can be toxic due to natural cyanide-forming compounds
  • Not recommended raw under any circumstances

Good to Know

Proper, prolonged cooking is essential and non-negotiable with cassava leaf; this is one herb where preparation safety matters more than any other property.

43 Plantain Leaf Ewe Ogede (Yoruba), Plantain/Banana Leaf Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Long used across Nigeria as natural food wrapping for moin-moin and other steamed dishes, plantain leaves also carry a quieter folk reputation for soothing skin and easing minor wounds.

Where to Find It

A common backyard plant nationwide; large fresh leaves are cut directly from the plant as needed.

Preparation

Fresh leaves are wilted briefly over heat to soften them, or crushed for topical application.

How to Use

Use softened leaves to wrap and steam foods, or apply the crushed leaf to minor cuts, stings or skin irritation.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally used to soothe insect bites and minor skin irritation
  • Used as a natural wrap that imparts flavour and moisture to steamed foods
  • Believed to have a mild cooling effect on skin
  • Occasionally used as a poultice for minor bruises

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Generally very safe; rare contact sensitivity possible
  • Not a substitute for proper wound care in deeper injuries

Good to Know

Beyond any medicinal use, plantain leaf's role as a natural, biodegradable food wrap for moin-moin and other dishes is a beloved part of everyday cooking.

44 Peppermint Ewe Minti (Yoruba, loanword) Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Though introduced from outside Africa, peppermint settled comfortably into Nigerian herbal tea culture, valued for its cooling effect on the stomach and mind alike.

Where to Find It

Grown in home gardens and increasingly on small commercial herb farms; sold fresh or dried in markets and supermarkets.

Preparation

Fresh or dried leaves are steeped in hot water for a few minutes to make tea.

How to Use

Drink the tea warm after meals for digestive comfort, or inhale the aroma to ease a stuffy nose.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally eases indigestion and bloating
  • Used to soothe nausea
  • Believed to help clear nasal congestion
  • A calming, refreshing everyday tea

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Can worsen heartburn/acid reflux in some people
  • Not usually given to very young infants

Good to Know

Peppermint tea after a heavy meal is a simple, widely available way to settle the stomach.

45 Rosemary Rosemary (no widely used local name) Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

An introduced Mediterranean herb, rosemary has been adopted into Nigerian gardens and kitchens for its fragrance, and increasingly for its reputation as a memory and hair-supporting herb.

Where to Find It

Grown in home and commercial herb gardens, especially in urban areas; sold fresh or dried in supermarkets and specialty herb stalls.

Preparation

Fresh or dried leaves are steeped for tea, infused into oil, or used directly in cooking.

How to Use

Add to roasted or grilled dishes, brew as tea, or infuse into oil for scalp and hair massage.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally used to support hair growth and scalp health
  • Believed to support memory and mental alertness
  • Used as a digestive aid after meals
  • Contains antioxidant compounds

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Concentrated essential oil should never be taken internally
  • Large medicinal doses are not recommended in pregnancy

Good to Know

Rosemary oil massaged into the scalp is an increasingly popular natural hair-care routine in Nigerian beauty circles.

46 Thyme Ewe Taimu (Yoruba, loanword) Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Thyme entered Nigerian kitchens as a seasoning staple for stews and grilled meats, and its reputation as a cough and cold remedy travelled alongside it.

Where to Find It

Grown in home and commercial gardens; sold fresh, dried, or as a common seasoning cube ingredient in every market.

Preparation

Fresh or dried leaves are steeped for tea, or added directly to cooking.

How to Use

Brew as a warm tea for cough and cold, or season soups, stews and grilled meats generously.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally used to ease cough and chest congestion
  • Believed to have antimicrobial properties
  • Used to support digestion
  • A common flavourful addition to immune-supporting teas

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Concentrated thyme oil is not for internal use
  • Large amounts may interact with blood-thinning or thyroid medication

Good to Know

Thyme is as much a kitchen staple in Nigeria as a home remedy, flavouring everything from jollof rice to grilled fish.

47 African Walnut Leaf (Ukpa) Ukpa (Igbo), Awusa (Yoruba), Tetracarpidium conophorum Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Best known for its hard-shelled roasted nuts sold by roadside vendors in the rainy season, the African walnut vine's leaves also carry a folk reputation for wound care and fertility support.

Where to Find It

A climbing vine native to southern Nigeria's forests; nuts are seasonal market favourites while leaves are gathered from the vine.

Preparation

Fresh leaves are boiled into a decoction; the nuts themselves are boiled or roasted before eating.

How to Use

Drink the leaf decoction in small amounts for traditional wellness support, or eat the boiled/roasted nut as a seasonal snack.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • The nut is a good source of plant protein and healthy fats
  • Leaves are traditionally used to support wound healing
  • Some traditional use of the leaf for fertility support
  • Believed to support general vitality

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Nuts must be cooked (boiled or roasted) before eating — raw walnuts can be difficult to digest
  • Limited formal research on leaf preparations; use in moderation

Good to Know

The roasted or boiled African walnut is a beloved rainy-season roadside snack in the south, sold hot in small paper cones.

48 Senna (Bush Tea) Senna, Local 'bush tea' varieties Leafy Greens & Garden Herbs +

History & Origin

Senna's leaves and pods have been used across Africa for centuries as one of the most reliable traditional remedies for constipation, its effect strong and well-recognised in folk practice.

Where to Find It

Grows as a wild shrub in savanna and farmland areas; dried leaves and pods are sold in local herb markets.

Preparation

Dried leaves or pods are steeped in hot water for a short time to make a mild-strength tea.

How to Use

Drink a small cup in the evening for occasional constipation relief, not as a daily habit.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • A well-established traditional remedy for occasional constipation
  • Used short-term to support regular bowel movement
  • Recognised across many cultures for its reliable laxative effect

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Not intended for regular, long-term use, as it can cause dependency and cramping
  • Can cause abdominal cramping if the dose is too strong
  • Not recommended in pregnancy or for children without guidance

Good to Know

Senna is genuinely effective but is best used occasionally and briefly rather than as an everyday tea, given its strong laxative action.

05 Ginger Jinja (pidgin), Chinkafa (Hausa), Ata-ile (Yoruba) Roots, Spices & Barks +

History & Origin

Ginger travelled to West Africa along ancient spice trade routes and was quickly absorbed into everyday cooking and home remedies for colds and stomach troubles.

Where to Find It

Cultivated widely in the Middle Belt, especially Kaduna and Nasarawa states; fresh rhizomes are sold in every food market year-round.

Preparation

The rhizome is peeled and sliced, grated fresh, or dried and ground into powder; it is also boiled to make a spicy tea.

How to Use

Steep sliced or grated ginger in hot water for tea, chew a small fresh piece, or add generously to soups and stews.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Eases nausea and morning sickness
  • Warms the body and soothes sore throats
  • Traditionally used for indigestion and bloating
  • Supports circulation

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Can worsen heartburn in sensitive people
  • Large amounts may thin the blood
  • May irritate the stomach if taken raw in excess

Good to Know

Ginger tea is one of the most common first responses to a cold or upset stomach in Nigerian households.

06 Garlic Ayu Ayu (Yoruba), Tafarnuwa (Hausa) Roots, Spices & Barks +

History & Origin

Long valued across West Africa as both food and medicine, garlic's pungent bulbs found their way into nearly every regional cuisine and household remedy cabinet.

Where to Find It

Grown in northern states and widely imported; fresh bulbs are a market staple sold alongside onions and ginger everywhere in the country.

Preparation

Cloves are crushed or chopped and used raw, cooked into food, or steeped in warm water or honey.

How to Use

Add crushed garlic to meals, chew a small raw clove, or mix crushed garlic with honey for a throat and cold remedy.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally supports heart and blood pressure health
  • Used for colds, cough and sore throat
  • Believed to have natural antimicrobial action
  • Supports general immunity

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Raw garlic can irritate the stomach lining
  • May increase bleeding risk with blood-thinning drugs
  • Can cause noticeable breath and body odour

Good to Know

Those scheduled for surgery are often advised to reduce garlic intake beforehand because of its blood-thinning effect.

08 Uziza Uziza (Igbo), Iyere-generic overlap (used both as leaf and seed spice) Roots, Spices & Barks +

History & Origin

Native to the West African rainforest belt, uziza's peppery leaves and seeds have flavoured Igbo pepper soups for generations while doubling as a warming remedy for colds.

Where to Find It

Wild-harvested and cultivated in southeastern Nigeria's forest zones; sold fresh as leaf bunches or dried as whole seed/berries in markets.

Preparation

Leaves are washed and sliced fresh; dried seeds/berries are ground into a peppery powder.

How to Use

Add sliced leaves or a pinch of ground seed to soups and pepper soup, especially for postnatal care and colds.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally warms the body after childbirth
  • Used for cough, catarrh and cold
  • Supports digestion and reduces bloating
  • Believed to ease joint and muscle aches

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Its peppery heat can irritate a sensitive stomach
  • Best used in culinary amounts rather than concentrated extracts

Good to Know

Uziza pepper soup is a well-known postpartum dish believed to help new mothers recover and produce milk.

09 Uda (Negro Pepper) Uda (Igbo), Eeru Alamo (Yoruba), Xylopia aethiopica Roots, Spices & Barks +

History & Origin

These slender, aromatic pods have flavoured southeastern Nigerian pepper soups and postnatal broths for centuries, valued as much for warmth as for taste.

Where to Find It

Harvested from wild and cultivated Xylopia trees in the southern rainforest belt; sold dried as whole pods in every local market.

Preparation

Dried pods are used whole in cooking or lightly crushed to release their aromatic oils; some grind them into powder.

How to Use

Add a few whole pods directly to soups, pepper soup, or a warm broth, especially during recovery after childbirth or illness.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally aids postpartum recovery and womb contraction
  • Used for cough and respiratory discomfort
  • Supports digestion and reduces gas
  • Believed to have warming, circulation-boosting effects

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Strong doses may be too warming for sensitive stomachs
  • Not typically recommended in large amounts during pregnancy before labour

Good to Know

Uda is a signature ingredient in southeastern Nigerian nursing-mother soups, usually paired with uziza and ginger.

24 Mango Bark & Leaf Ewe Mongoro (Yoruba), Mangoro Roots, Spices & Barks +

History & Origin

Mango trees shade compounds across Nigeria, and long before the fruit ripens each season, its bark and young leaves have served as an everyday remedy for stomach and tooth troubles.

Where to Find It

A widespread fruit tree found in nearly every compound and roadside; bark and leaves are gathered directly and rarely need market purchase.

Preparation

Bark is scraped and boiled into a firm decoction; young leaves are boiled similarly for a milder tea.

How to Use

Drink the bark decoction for diarrhoea or as a mouth rinse for toothache; the leaf tea is used more gently for coughs.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally used for diarrhoea and dysentery
  • Used as a mouth rinse for toothache and gum discomfort
  • Believed to ease cough and mild respiratory discomfort
  • Some traditional use for fever support

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Bark decoctions are astringent and may cause constipation if overused
  • Concentrated bark preparations are not recommended for children

Good to Know

Mango bark is one of the older, less-discussed remedies, still used in many rural households alongside more familiar leaf teas.

26 Turmeric Ata Ile Pupa (Yoruba, 'red ground pepper') Roots, Spices & Barks +

History & Origin

Turmeric arrived via ancient trade with South Asia and settled comfortably into Nigerian cooking and traditional medicine, its golden colour becoming a marker of warmth and healing.

Where to Find It

Cultivated in parts of the Middle Belt and south-west; fresh rhizomes and dried powder are widely available in markets.

Preparation

Fresh rhizome is grated or sliced; dried rhizome is ground into the familiar bright yellow powder.

How to Use

Add powder or grated fresh turmeric to soups, stews, or golden milk (turmeric mixed with warm milk and pepper).

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally used to ease joint discomfort and inflammation
  • Believed to support digestion
  • Used for skin conditions when applied as a paste
  • Rich in antioxidant compounds (curcumin)

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Can interact with blood-thinning medication at high doses
  • May cause stomach upset in very large amounts
  • Can stain skin and clothing bright yellow

Good to Know

Turmeric is best absorbed by the body when taken alongside black pepper and a little fat, a pairing already common in local cooking.

27 Cloves Kanafuru (Hausa/Yoruba, loanword) Roots, Spices & Barks +

History & Origin

Cloves reached Nigeria through long-distance spice trade and were embraced for both flavour and their distinctive numbing, warming effect, especially for tooth pain.

Where to Find It

Not grown locally in significant quantity; dried whole cloves are imported and sold widely in spice sections of every market.

Preparation

Whole cloves are used directly, ground into powder, or steeped in hot water or oil.

How to Use

Chew a whole clove or hold it against an aching tooth, add to spiced teas, or use clove-infused oil topically.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Traditionally used to numb toothache
  • Used for digestive discomfort and bloating
  • Believed to have antimicrobial properties
  • Added to teas for warming, cold-easing effect

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Clove oil is very strong and can irritate gums or skin if undiluted
  • Large amounts may thin the blood

Good to Know

A single clove held against a painful tooth remains one of the most common quick home remedies for toothache in Nigeria.

28 Cinnamon Oloorun (Yoruba, loanword/associative name) Roots, Spices & Barks +

History & Origin

Brought in through the same spice routes as cloves, cinnamon became a favoured warming spice in teas and porridges, valued for its comforting aroma and traditional health reputation.

Where to Find It

Not native to Nigeria; imported cinnamon sticks and powder are sold in spice markets and supermarkets nationwide.

Preparation

History & Origin

Coconut palms line Nigeria's coastal south, and every part of the fruit, water, flesh and pressed oil, has found its way into both the kitchen and the medicine cabinet.

Where to Find It

Grown along the coastal and southern regions; whole coconuts and pressed oil are sold in markets nationwide.

Preparation

Coconut water is drunk straight from young nuts; the flesh is pressed (traditionally by hand) into oil.

How to Use

Drink coconut water fresh, or apply the oil topically to skin and hair, or cook with it in small amounts.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Coconut water is a natural, hydrating electrolyte drink
  • Coconut oil is used to moisturise skin and hair
  • Traditionally used to soothe dry or irritated skin
  • Believed to support healthy hair growth when massaged into the scalp

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Coconut oil is high in saturated fat, so dietary use should be moderate
  • Coconut water in very large amounts can affect potassium and electrolyte balance

Good to Know

Fresh coconut water, drunk straight from the nut, is one of Nigeria's most popular natural rehydration drinks, especially in hot weather.

40 Shea Tree (Ori) Ori (Yoruba), Kade (Hausa), Shea Butter Tree Oils & Extracts +

History & Origin

Native to Nigeria's savanna belt, the shea tree has supplied women's cooperatives with butter for skin and cooking for generations, its production a deeply rooted local craft.

Where to Find It

Wild-harvested from savanna trees across the Middle Belt and north; raw and refined shea butter is sold nationwide.

Preparation

Nuts are boiled, dried, crushed and traditionally hand-kneaded in water until the butter separates and solidifies.

How to Use

Apply the butter directly to skin, hair and scalp; unrefined shea butter is also used in some cooking traditions.

Healing & Wellness Properties

  • Deeply moisturises dry skin and hair
  • Traditionally used to soothe minor burns and irritation
  • Believed to help ease joint and muscle discomfort when massaged in
  • Used to protect skin from harsh weather

Side Effects & Cautions

  • Rare nut-allergy related skin reactions are possible
  • Unrefined butter has a strong natural scent some may find heavy

Good to Know

Shea butter production remains an important livelihood for many women's cooperatives across northern and Middle Belt Nigeria.
